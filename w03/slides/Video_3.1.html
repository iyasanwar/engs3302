A die rolled 10,000 times (computer simulation)

Probability is a long-run fraction

An experiment has an uncertain result. An event is a set of results, like “the die shows 6”. P(A) is the fraction of times A happens, when we repeat the experiment many, many times.

rolls 10,000   sixes so far 0.169

The line does not get closer at every roll. After 1,000 rolls it was 0.174, further from 1/6 than after 100. The swings get smaller.

Why we care: we estimate probabilities from training data. A class with few samples gives an estimate we cannot trust much.

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1all outcomes add up to 1P(not A) = 1 − P(A)
Click 1: watch the computer roll a die 10,000 times; the line is the fraction of sixes so far: 0.100 after 10, 0.160 after 100, 0.174 after 1,000, 0.169 after 10,000. Click 2: it does not approach at every roll; the swings get smaller. Click 3: we estimate from training data; small classes give shaky estimates. Click 4: three rules.

Counting inside a row or a column

Conditional probability

P(A | B), “A given B”: the probability of A when we know that B happened. Keep only the row or column you know, and count inside it.

LIGHTDARKTOTAL
sea bass56460
salmon33740
total5941100
P(light | sea bass) =56÷60=0.933
P(sea bass | light) =56÷59=0.949

We know it is a sea bass: keep the sea bass row. 56 of its 60 fish are light.

We know it is light: keep the light column. 56 of its 59 fish are sea bass.

joint:  P(sea bass, light) = 56 / 100  ÷ all the fish

conditional:  P(light | sea bass) = 56 / 60  ÷ the sea bass only

0.933 and 0.949 are close today only because sea bass are common. In Video 3.2 they will be far apart. Never swap them. The classifier needs P(sea bass | light).

Click 1: P(light | sea bass): keep the sea bass row: 56/60 = 0.933. Click 2: P(sea bass | light): keep the light column: 56/59 = 0.949. Two different questions; the second is the classifier's question. Click 3: same numerator, different denominator: ask yourself, from which group am I counting? Click 4: close today by chance; never swap them.

Independence

Independent events

Two events are independent if knowing one does not change the probability of the other. Then P(A, B) = P(A) × P(B)

Two fish, caught one after the other:

Are lightness and species independent? Test it:

if independent0.60 × 0.59 = 0.354
→ about 35 in 100
the table56 light sea bass
in 100

They are not independent: 56 is far from 35. Good news. If lightness did not depend on species, it could never help us classify.

Click 1: two fish, each sea bass with probability 0.6, independent: 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.36, the corner of the square. Click 2: if lightness and species were independent, P(sea bass, light) would be 0.60 × 0.59 = 0.354: about 35 fish in 100. The table has 56. Click 3: not independent, and that is good news for classification. Next video: how to turn P(light | sea bass) around.
