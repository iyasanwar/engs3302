The normal density

The normal curve: centre and spread

μ (centre) 7.0

σ (spread) 1.0

highest point 0.399

area 1.000

Many measurements give a bell-shaped histogram. The normal (Gaussian) curve describes it with two numbers: μ, the centre, and σ, the spread.

μ moves the curve left and right. The shape stays the same.

A larger σ makes the curve wider, so it gets lower: the area must stay 1.

p(x) = (0.3989 / σ) · e−z²/2  z = (x − μ) / σ

Written N(μ, σ²) as in Duda. In Python, norm.pdf takes σ, not σ².

About 68% of the area lies within 1 σ of μ, about 95% within 2 σ. Sea bass N(7, 1): P(6 < X < 8) = 0.683, P(5 < X < 9) = 0.954.

The normal curve: two numbers, μ and σ. Click 1: μ moves it (7 to 4 and back). Click 2: σ widens it and lowers it (σ = 2: peak 0.199; σ = 0.5: peak 0.798); the area stays 1. Click 3: the formula: you use it, you do not derive it. z is the same z as in Week 2. N(μ, σ²) as in Duda; norm.pdf takes σ. Click 4: 68% within one σ, 95% within two: 0.683 and 0.954 for sea bass N(7, 1).

The Week 4 models: salmon N(4, 1), sea bass N(7, 1). A fish arrives with lightness 6.

Sea bass: z = (6 − 7) / 1 = −1, so e−1/2 = 0.6065.

Salmon: z = (6 − 4) / 1 = 2, so e−2 = 0.1353. 6 is two σ from the salmon centre, so the curve is low there.

6 fits the sea bass model about 4.5 times better. This is not yet P(sea bass | 6): we also need how common each class is (Video 3.2). That is Week 4.

Click 1: sea bass: z = −1, e^(−0.5) = 0.6065, × 0.3989 = 0.242. Click 2: salmon: z = 2, e^(−2) = 0.1353, × 0.3989 = 0.054. Click 3: about 4.5 times higher for sea bass: 6 is one σ from the sea bass centre and two from the salmon centre. Not yet the probability of sea bass: we also need how common each class is. May we compare densities?

Why we can compare densities

width 1

A density is not a probability. So may we compare 0.242 with 0.054? Take a strip around 6 under each curve.

A thin strip is almost a rectangle: probability ≈ density × width. The probabilities shrink with the width. The ratio stays at 4.48.

Width 1 gives 3.99: a wide strip is not a rectangle. So: comparing two densities = comparing two thin strips of the same width.

p(x | ω) is the class-conditional density: the density of x for the fish of one class. Week 4 compares it, with how common each class is, to decide.

May we compare 0.242 with 0.054? Take a strip around 6. Width 1: 0.2417 vs 0.0606, ratio 3.99. Click 1: width 0.1: 0.0242 vs 0.0054, ratio 4.48. Click 2: width 0.01: ten times smaller, same ratio. Click 3: the wide strip is not a rectangle, so the ratio there is 3.99. Click 4: p(x | ω), the class-conditional density; Week 4's Bayes decision.

In Python

import numpy as np; from scipy.stats import norm
rolls = np.random.default_rng(0).integers(1, 7, 10000)
(rolls == 6).mean()0.169a long-run fraction
0.933*0.05 / (0.933*0.05 + 0.075*0.95)0.396Bayes’ rule: one line
norm.pdf(6, loc=7, scale=1)0.242
norm.cdf(8, 7, 1) - norm.cdf(6, 7, 1)0.683
plt.hist(x, bins=13, density=True)area 1

norm.pdf is a height (a density). norm.cdf is an area to the left of a point, so a difference of two is the probability of an interval.

Each click runs one line. The die: 0.169. Bayes: 0.396. norm.pdf: a height, 0.242. norm.cdf: an area; the difference is the probability of an interval, 0.683. plt.hist with density=True draws a density histogram with area 1. You will run all of this in the lab.

Remember

Probability is a fraction; a density is a height

Probability is a fraction. In a table, count inside the row or column you know.

Bayes’ rule turns a conditional around. How common each class is changes the answer.

A continuous feature has a density. A probability is the area under it, not its height.
