Now.So.Next.

The idea: choose the curve that makes the fish we saw as likely as possible. Keep σ = 1 for now.

μ = 3.0: one fish in the thin tail, the one at 5.6, makes the whole product small.

μ = 3.5: better. The product is about 5 times larger.

μ = 3.88: the largest product. We call it the likelihood = a product of densities

μ = 4.5: now the fish at 2.5 is in the tail. The winner, 3.88, is exactly the average of the five.

Maximum likelihood

The answer: the average

μ̂ = 1n Σ xₖthe average
σ̂² = 1n Σ (xₖ − μ̂)²the average squared distance
μ̂3.88
σ̂² ÷ n1.206
σ̂1.10
x = np.array([2.5, 3.0, 3.8, 4.5, 5.6])
np.mean(x)         # 3.88
np.var(x)          # 1.206
np.var(x, ddof=1)  # 1.507

np.var: n. pandas, np.cov: n − 1.

Now.So.Next.

The rule for μ: the plain average of the training values.

Now free σ, which we kept at 1. The same idea gives σ²: the average squared distance from μ̂.

For the five salmon: the curve that fits them best. One line of NumPy gives both numbers.

Divide by n, not n − 1. With many fish the two are almost equal.

We tried only four guesses. How do we know the average beats every guess?
