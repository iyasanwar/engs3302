From counts to density

A histogram counts the penguins in each 5 mm bin. The blue bar holds 62 penguins.

Step 1: divide the count by the width. This is the count density: penguins per mm.

Step 2: divide by all 342 penguins. This is the probability density: the fraction per mm.

Each step on the axis is 1 mm, so 0.0363 is also the probability of one mm, on average. (Per metre the density is 36.3; one mm is still 0.0363.)

Height × width gives back the fraction: 0.0363 × 5 = 0.181. All the areas add up to 1.

A density is a count per size, like people per km². Click 1: the 62 comes from the height, the 5 from the width: 62 ÷ 5 = 12.4 penguins per mm. This is the count density. Click 2: ÷ all 342 penguins: 0.0363 per mm. This is the probability density. The question

Why can a density be larger than 1?

Answer

The axis is 10 times narrower, so the curve must be 10 times taller to keep its area at 1. The probabilities stay the same: P(6 < X < 8) = P(0.6 < X < 0.8) = 0.683.

Both panels use the same height axis. Click 1: the same fish, lightness divided by 10. Squeezed only, the curve would hold area 0.1. To keep area 1 it grows 10 times: peak 0.399 → 3.99, above the line at 1. Click 2: every probability stays the same. A density can be any positive number; a probability never exceeds 1. In metres: 12 400 penguins per metre in Worked example 3.5.
